Section 02 Disease background

Schizophrenia and bipolar disorder

Clinical background on the two conditions in which olanzapine is used. Product information, evidence and prescribing details are in the sections that follow.

Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is a psychiatric disorder that can affect how a person perceives reality, thinks, experiences emotions and interacts with others. Its presentation varies between individuals, and symptoms can change over time.

Treatment aims to manage symptoms, prevent relapse where possible, support everyday functioning and address the individual's broader healthcare needs.

2.2

The neurobiology of schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is associated with complex changes in brain function and neurotransmission. One widely discussed model links three interconnected hypotheses: excess dopamine D2 activity in the mesolimbic pathway, hypofunction of glutamate NMDA receptors, and hyperfunction of cortical serotonin 5-HT2A receptors.5

In this model, NMDA receptor hypofunction and 5-HT2A hyperfunction can each lead, further downstream, to mesolimbic dopamine hyperactivity, which is associated with positive symptoms. Negative and cognitive symptoms are thought to involve other circuits.5 No single neurotransmitter imbalance fully explains schizophrenia or accounts for every symptom an individual experiences.

2.3

Treatment across the course of schizophrenia

Treatment needs can change over time. The following phases are a useful clinical framework rather than a rigid sequence that applies identically to every patient.

Supporting continuity of care

Follow-up should consider treatment response, tolerability, daily functioning, social support and the individual's ability to continue the agreed treatment plan.

Adherence may be affected by adverse effects, the nature of the illness, treatment preferences and practical barriers. Explore these collaboratively rather than attributing them to the patient alone.

Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder is characterized by episodes of mood disturbance, including mania or hypomania and depression, depending on the type of bipolar disorder. Episodes can affect energy, sleep, judgement, behaviour and everyday functioning.

Treatment decisions depend on the current episode, previous clinical history, treatment response, comorbidities and the individual's needs.

Understanding mood episodes

2.5

Treatment objectives

Treatment objectives depend on the phase of illness.

  • Acute managementAddress the current episode and associated clinical risks.
  • StabilizationConsolidate improvement and review tolerability.
  • Recurrence preventionSupport sustained clinical stability following a treatment response.

These objectives should be individualized. Treatment for an acute manic episode should not automatically be assumed to provide adequate management for every subsequent mood episode.

Section 03 Mechanism of action

Understanding the pharmacology of olanzapine

A broad receptor-binding profile contributes to its pharmacological effects.

Olanzapine is an antipsychotic, antimanic and mood-stabilising agent with activity across several neurotransmitter receptor systems.1

Its pharmacological profile includes activity at serotonin, dopamine, muscarinic, histamine and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors. The relative contribution of individual receptor interactions to clinical efficacy and adverse effects should be interpreted carefully.

3.1

Receptor-binding profile

Select a receptor family to see what it is linked to.

Dopamine receptor activity D1, D2, D3, D4, D5

Olanzapine has affinity for dopamine receptors, including D2 receptors.1

Dopamine receptor antagonism is relevant to its antipsychotic activity. Dopaminergic signalling is involved in multiple brain functions, and the relationship between receptor activity and individual symptom domains is complex.

Serotonin receptor activity 5-HT2A/2C, 5-HT3, 5-HT6

Olanzapine also has affinity for serotonin receptors, including 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C receptors.1

Its serotonin and dopamine receptor activity contributes to its overall pharmacological profile. However, the clinical effects of olanzapine cannot be attributed to one receptor interaction alone.

Histamine H1 receptors H1

Relevant to effects such as somnolence and increased appetite.13

Muscarinic receptors M1–M5

Relevant to anticholinergic adverse effects.13

Alpha-1 adrenergic receptors α1

Relevant to orthostatic hypotension and dizziness.13

These interactions help explain why treatment selection and monitoring must consider both therapeutic objectives and potential adverse effects.

4.4

Guidelines

Guidelines place olanzapine among several options rather than ahead of them. Recommendations depend on the phase of illness, patient preference and how well the adverse-effect profile suits the individual.

APA practice guideline, 20216

Schizophrenia
  • Treat with an antipsychotic and monitor effectiveness and side effects (1A).
  • Continue antipsychotic treatment after symptoms improve (1A), preferably with the same medicine (2B).
  • Clozapine for treatment-resistant schizophrenia (1B).
  • Long-acting injectable if preferred or adherence is poor (2B).
  • Individual antipsychotics are not ranked.

BAP guidelines, 20167

Bipolar disorder
  • Severe mania: haloperidol, olanzapine, risperidone and quetiapine described as particularly effective.
  • Long term: olanzapine is among the agents shown to prevent manic relapse; lithium is recommended as initial monotherapy.
  • Weight gain is a prominent adverse effect of olanzapine.

WPA review, 20088

Schizophrenia
  • Apart from clozapine, no consistent efficacy differences between second-generation antipsychotics.
  • Olanzapine is associated with more weight gain and metabolic adverse effects than most comparators.

WHO Essential Medicines List, 20239

Psychotic and bipolar disorders
  • Olanzapine listed as a therapeutic alternative to risperidone (psychotic disorders) and to quetiapine (bipolar disorders).

The daily dose range is 5–20 mg, individualised for each patient. Dose adjustments should be based on clinical assessment. An increase above the recommended starting dose should occur only after appropriate clinical reassessment and generally at intervals of not less than 24 hours.1

For patients who have been receiving olanzapine for a manic episode, therapy for preventing recurrence continues at the same dose. If a new manic, mixed or depressive episode occurs, olanzapine treatment should be continued (with dose optimisation as needed), with supplementary therapy to treat mood symptoms, as clinically indicated.1

Olanzapine can be given without regard to meals, as absorption is not affected by food.

Special populations

Older patients (65 years and over)

A lower starting dose (5 mg/day) is not routinely indicated but should be considered when clinical factors warrant.1

Renal and/or hepatic impairment

A lower starting dose (5 mg) should be considered. In moderate hepatic insufficiency (cirrhosis, Child-Pugh Class A or B), the starting dose should be 5 mg and only increased with caution.1

Factors that may slow metabolism

When more than one factor is present (female gender, geriatric age, non-smoking status), consider decreasing the starting dose. Dose escalation should be conservative.1

Smokers

Starting dose and range need not be routinely altered. Metabolism may be induced by smoking; clinical monitoring is recommended and a dose increase may be considered if necessary.1

Children and adolescents

Not recommended below 18 years of age due to a lack of data on safety and efficacy.1

5.3

Stopping treatment

Acute symptoms such as sweating, insomnia, tremor, anxiety, nausea or vomiting have been reported rarely when olanzapine is stopped abruptly.1

Gradual tapering of the dose should be considered when discontinuing olanzapine. Treatment changes should be managed by the treating healthcare professional.1

6.2

Important warnings and precautions

During antipsychotic treatment, improvement in the patient's clinical condition may take several days to some weeks. Patients should be closely monitored during this period.1

Not recommended

Dementia-related psychosis and/or behavioural disturbances

Olanzapine is not recommended for patients with dementia-related psychosis and/or behavioural disturbances because of an increase in mortality and the risk of cerebrovascular accident. In placebo-controlled trials (6–12 weeks) in elderly patients (mean age 78 years):1

3.5% vs 1.5%incidence of death, olanzapine vs placebo (2-fold increase)
1.3% vs 0.4%cerebrovascular adverse events, olanzapine vs placebo (3-fold increase)

The efficacy of olanzapine was not established in these trials.

Key warnings

Metabolic effects

Olanzapine treatment has been associated with weight gain and changes in glucose and lipid parameters. Hyperglycaemia and the development or worsening of diabetes, occasionally associated with ketoacidosis or coma, have been reported uncommonly, including some fatal cases.1

Assess relevant baseline risk factors and monitor weight, blood glucose and lipid parameters as set out in the monitoring schedule. See monitoring.

Somnolence and dizziness

Somnolence and dizziness may occur. Patients should be cautioned about operating machinery, including motor vehicles.1

Sedation and orthostatic hypotension should be considered when assessing falls risk, concomitant medication and individual tolerability.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a potentially life-threatening condition associated with antipsychotic medicines. Clinical manifestations include hyperpyrexia, muscle rigidity, altered mental status and autonomic instability.1

If a patient develops signs and symptoms indicative of NMS, or presents with unexplained high fever without additional clinical manifestations of NMS, all antipsychotic medicines, including olanzapine, must be discontinued.

Tardive dyskinesia and other movement disorders

Movement-related adverse effects may occur. The risk of tardive dyskinesia increases with long-term exposure; patients should be assessed for relevant symptoms during treatment.1

If signs or symptoms of tardive dyskinesia appear, dose reduction or discontinuation should be considered. These symptoms can temporarily deteriorate or even arise after discontinuation of treatment.

Cardiovascular and orthostatic effects

Orthostatic hypotension and dizziness may occur. Caution is required in patients with relevant cardiovascular risk factors or when medicines with potential interactions are co-administered. Blood pressure should be measured periodically in patients over 65 years.1

Hepatic and haematological considerations

Transient, asymptomatic elevations of hepatic aminotransferases (ALT, AST) have been seen commonly, especially in early treatment. Follow-up is important in patients with elevated liver enzymes, signs of hepatic impairment, limited hepatic functional reserve or concomitant use of potentially hepatotoxic medicines. If hepatitis is diagnosed, olanzapine treatment should be discontinued.1

Caution is also required in patients with low leukocyte or neutrophil counts, medicines known to cause neutropenia, a history of drug-induced bone marrow depression, or hypereosinophilic or myeloproliferative conditions. Neutropenia has been reported commonly when olanzapine and valproate are used concomitantly.

Other warnings

Parkinson's disease

Not recommended for dopamine agonist-associated psychosis in Parkinson's disease; worsening of parkinsonian symptoms and hallucinations was reported very commonly.1

QT interval

Caution with medicines known to increase QTc, especially in the elderly and in congenital long QT syndrome, congestive heart failure, heart hypertrophy, hypokalaemia or hypomagnesaemia.1

Thromboembolism

Venous thromboembolism has been reported uncommonly. Identify risk factors such as immobilisation and take preventive measures.1

Seizures

Use cautiously in patients with a history of seizures or factors that may lower the seizure threshold.1

Sudden cardiac death

Reported post-marketing. In an observational study, the risk with olanzapine was approximately twice that in patients not using antipsychotics, and comparable to pooled atypical antipsychotics.1

Anticholinergic activity

Caution in prostatic hypertrophy, paralytic ileus and related conditions.1

General CNS activity

Caution with alcohol and other centrally acting medicines. Olanzapine may antagonise the effects of dopamine agonists.1

Lactose

Patients with rare hereditary galactose intolerance, total lactase deficiency or glucose-galactose malabsorption should not take this medicine.1

6.6

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Because human experience is limited, olanzapine should be used in pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the foetus.1

Newborns exposed to antipsychotics, including olanzapine, during the third trimester are at risk of adverse reactions including extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms, and should be monitored carefully.

Olanzapine is excreted in breast milk. Patients should be advised not to breastfeed an infant if they are taking olanzapine.
