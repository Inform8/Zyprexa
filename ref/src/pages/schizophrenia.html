Disease area

Understanding schizophrenia

A multidimensional condition requiring individualized, ongoing care.

Schizophrenia is a psychiatric disorder that can affect how a person perceives reality, thinks, experiences emotions and interacts with others. Its presentation varies between individuals, and symptoms can change over time.

Treatment aims to manage symptoms, prevent relapse where possible, support everyday functioning and address the individual's broader healthcare needs.

Neurobiology

The neurobiology of schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is associated with complex changes in brain function and neurotransmission. One widely discussed model links three interconnected hypotheses: excess dopamine D2 activity in the mesolimbic pathway, hypofunction of glutamate NMDA receptors, and hyperfunction of cortical serotonin 5-HT2A receptors.{{c:stahl}}

In this model, NMDA receptor hypofunction and 5-HT2A hyperfunction can each lead, further downstream, to mesolimbic dopamine hyperactivity, which is associated with positive symptoms. Negative and cognitive symptoms are thought to involve other circuits.{{c:stahl}} No single neurotransmitter imbalance fully explains schizophrenia or accounts for every symptom an individual experiences.

Olanzapine

The role of olanzapine

Olanzapine is an antipsychotic medicine indicated for schizophrenia. The European product information states that it is effective in maintaining clinical improvement during continuation therapy in patients who have shown an initial treatment response.{{c:1:4.1}}

The SmPC does not state the duration of that continuation study, and it does not cite a separate primary publication for it. The acute programme in positive and negative symptoms likewise has no duration in SmPC 5.1. The published North American trial cited on the clinical evidence page (Beasley et al. 1996) was 6 weeks.{{c:beasley}}

Clinical decisions should consider the individual's symptoms, previous treatment response, comorbidities, preferences and potential adverse effects.

Follow-up

Supporting continuity of care

Schizophrenia management extends beyond symptom assessment. Follow-up should consider treatment response, tolerability, daily functioning, social support and the individual's ability to continue the agreed treatment plan.

Treatment adherence may be affected by multiple factors, including adverse effects, the nature of the illness, treatment preferences and practical barriers. These factors should be explored collaboratively rather than attributed to the patient alone.

The APA practice guideline recommends continuing antipsychotic medication once symptoms have improved, and suggests continuing the same antipsychotic where it has been effective. A long-acting injectable may be considered if the patient prefers it or has a history of poor or uncertain adherence.{{c:apa}}
