Understanding bipolar disorder

Recognizing mood episodes and defining appropriate treatment objectives.

Bipolar disorder is characterized by episodes of mood disturbance, including mania or hypomania and depression, depending on the type of bipolar disorder. Episodes can affect energy, sleep, judgement, behaviour and everyday functioning.

Treatment decisions depend on the current episode, previous clinical history, treatment response, comorbidities and the individual's needs.

Treatment

Treatment objectives

Treatment objectives depend on the phase of illness.

  • Acute managementAddress the current episode and associated clinical risks.
  • StabilizationConsolidate improvement and review tolerability.
  • Recurrence preventionSupport sustained clinical stability following a treatment response.

These objectives should be individualized. Treatment for an acute manic episode should not automatically be assumed to provide adequate management for every subsequent mood episode.

Olanzapine in bipolar disorder · EU SmPC 4.1

Oral olanzapine is indicated for

  • Treatment of moderate to severe manic episodes.
  • Prevention of recurrence in patients with bipolar disorder whose manic episode has responded to olanzapine.

Not an indication in the EU SmPC

  • Bipolar depression (oral monotherapy)

The product information also describes use in combination therapy for acute mania. The choice of monotherapy or combination therapy depends on the approved prescribing information and the patient's clinical circumstances.{{c:1:4.2}}

Clinical evidence

Evidence for acute mania

Clinical studies described in the product information evaluated olanzapine in patients experiencing manic or mixed episodes associated with bipolar disorder.

All acute mania study descriptions {{arrow}}
  • Versus placebo and valproate semisodium (divalproex). Olanzapine showed greater reduction in manic symptoms than placebo and divalproex over 3 weeks in the studies described.{{c:1:5.1}}
  • Published placebo-controlled trial. In a 3-week trial in 139 patients with a manic or mixed episode, olanzapine (mean modal dose 14.9 mg/day) reduced Young Mania Rating Scale scores by a mean 10.3 points versus 4.9 with placebo (p = 0.02); 48.6% versus 24.2% of patients responded (p = 0.004). Somnolence, dizziness, dry mouth and weight gain were more frequent with olanzapine.{{c:tohen}} Trial details
  • Combination with lithium or valproate. In patients treated with lithium or valproate for at least 2 weeks, adding olanzapine 10 mg resulted in a greater reduction in manic symptoms than lithium or valproate alone after six weeks.{{c:1:5.1}}

These findings must be interpreted in the context of the individual study designs, populations, comparators and endpoints. They should not be generalized to all bipolar presentations.

Clinical evidence · 12-month studies

Evidence for recurrence prevention

Two 12-month studies enrolled patients whose manic episode had remitted, then randomised them to continue olanzapine or to a comparator. Both used bipolar recurrence as the primary endpoint.{{c:1:5.1}}

Study A · Olanzapine versus placebo

The European SmPC describes a 12-month study in patients who had achieved remission from a manic episode with olanzapine. Patients randomised to continue olanzapine had statistically significant superiority over placebo for the primary endpoint of bipolar recurrence. Olanzapine also showed a statistically significant advantage over placebo in preventing either recurrence into mania or recurrence into depression.{{c:1:5.1}}

Sample size, baseline characteristics, blinding, inclusion criteria, the exact definition of recurrence and numerical recurrence rates are not stated in EU SmPC 5.1, so they are not shown. A primary publication for Study A was not supplied. Detailed charts for the studies that do report numbers are on the clinical evidence page.

Long-term combination therapy

In an 18-month co-therapy study in manic or mixed episode patients stabilised with olanzapine plus a mood stabiliser (lithium or valproate), long-term olanzapine co-therapy with lithium or valproate was not statistically significantly superior to lithium or valproate alone in delaying bipolar recurrence, defined according to syndromic (diagnostic) criteria.{{c:1:5.1}}

Added from SmPC 5.1 for balance: the 30.0% vs 38.3% (p = 0.055) figures, the mania/depression recurrence statement, "over 3 weeks", "for at least 2 weeks" and the 18-month co-therapy result. Guidelines

Where olanzapine sits in guidelines

The British Association for Psychopharmacology guideline lists olanzapine among the oral dopamine antagonists that are particularly effective in severe mania, alongside haloperidol, risperidone and quetiapine.{{c:bap}}

For long-term treatment, the same guideline recommends considering lithium as initial monotherapy. Olanzapine is among the agents shown to prevent manic relapse; the guideline identifies lithium, quetiapine and lamotrigine as the agents with convincing evidence for preventing depressive relapse. It also notes that weight gain is a prominent adverse effect of olanzapine.{{c:bap}}

The WHO Model List of Essential Medicines includes olanzapine as a therapeutic alternative to quetiapine for bipolar disorders.{{c:eml}}

Safety

Safety considerations during treatment

Weight gain, increased appetite, changes in glucose and lipid parameters, somnolence and other adverse reactions should be considered when selecting and monitoring treatment.

Regular follow-up allows clinicians to evaluate clinical response, tolerability and relevant physical-health parameters.
